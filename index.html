Foundation

What is Ocular Inflammation?

Before prescribing anti-inflammatory agents, an OA student must understand the biology of inflammation in the eye — its triggers, mediators, and consequences on ocular structures.

🔥 The Inflammatory Cascade

When ocular tissue is damaged or exposed to antigens, phospholipase A₂ is activated in cell membranes. This releases arachidonic acid, which is metabolised through two key pathways:

  • COX pathway → Prostaglandins & Thromboxanes — cause vasodilation, pain, and vascular permeability
  • LOX pathway → Leukotrienes — attract neutrophils, cause chemotaxis and further tissue damage

👁️ Why the Eye is Unique

The eye has immune privilege — the blood-ocular barrier limits systemic immune access. When this barrier is breached:

  • Breakdown of the blood-aqueous barrier causes flare and cells in the anterior chamber
  • Leakage at the macula leads to cystoid macular oedema (CME)
  • Unchecked inflammation → synechiae, cataract, glaucoma, phthisis

🧬 Key Inflammatory Mediators

  • Prostaglandin E₂ & PGI₂ — vasodilation, pain, IOP fluctuation
  • Leukotriene B₄ — neutrophil chemotaxis
  • Histamine — immediate hypersensitivity, itch, redness
  • IL-1, TNF-α — amplify inflammation via NF-κB
  • PAF (Platelet Activating Factor) — potent pro-inflammatory phospholipid

📋 Types of Ocular Inflammation

  • Uveitis — anterior, intermediate, posterior, panuveitis
  • Allergic conjunctivitis — seasonal, perennial, vernal, atopic
  • Scleritis / Episcleritis
  • Post-surgical inflammation — after cataract, refractive, vitreoretinal surgery
  • Corneal graft rejection
  • Dry eye disease — inflammatory component
Classification

Drug Classes Overview

Ocular anti-inflammatory agents are broadly divided based on their mechanism and chemical nature. Each class has a specific role, onset, potency, and risk profile.

📌 Routes of Administration in Ophthalmic Practice

  • Topical drops/ointment — most common; acts locally with limited systemic absorption
  • Periocular injection — subconjunctival, sub-Tenon, posterior juxtascleral; bypasses corneal barrier
  • Intravitreal injection — direct posterior segment delivery; triamcinolone, dexamethasone implant
  • Systemic oral — for severe uveitis, scleritis; prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day
  • IV pulse — methylprednisolone 1 g/day × 3 for severe posterior uveitis
  • Sustained-release implants — Ozurdex (DEX), Iluvien (FAc), Yutiq (FAc)
Corticosteroids

Mechanism & Pharmacology
of Ocular Steroids

Corticosteroids remain the gold standard for treating ocular inflammation. They act at multiple levels of the inflammatory cascade, making them far more potent than NSAIDs.

💧 Pharmacokinetics of Topical Steroids

  • Absorption: Penetration depends on lipophilicity, vehicle, and corneal epithelial integrity. Suspension forms (e.g. prednisolone acetate) penetrate better than solutions
  • Distribution: Reaches aqueous humour, iris, ciliary body. Posterior penetration is poor from topical route
  • Metabolism: Local esterases in the cornea activate prodrugs (e.g. loteprednol etabonate). Soft steroids undergo predictable local metabolism → low systemic levels
  • Elimination: Via nasolacrimal drainage → systemic absorption via nasal mucosa (minimise by punctal occlusion)

⚖️ Potency Spectrum

High Potency

Prednisolone acetate 1%, Dexamethasone 0.1%, Betamethasone — used for active anterior uveitis, corneal graft rejection, severe post-op inflammation

Medium Potency

Fluorometholone 0.1%, Medrysone — preferred for surface disease, allergic conjunctivitis; lower IOP risk

Soft Steroids (Retrometabolic)

Loteprednol etabonate, Rimexolone — designed to undergo predictable local metabolism; lowest risk of IOP elevation and cataract

🩺 Clinical Indications for Ocular Steroids

NSAIDs

Non-Steroidal Anti-inflammatory
Drugs (NSAIDs)

Topical NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, preventing prostaglandin synthesis. They are steroid-sparing agents particularly valued for post-surgical use and pain management.

⚙️ Mechanism of Action

NSAIDs competitively and irreversibly inhibit COX-1 and COX-2 enzymes. This prevents conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandins and thromboxanes. Unlike steroids, NSAIDs do NOT inhibit phospholipase A₂ — leukotrienes continue to be formed. This is why NSAIDs are less potent than steroids for severe inflammation.

🎯 COX-1 vs COX-2 in the Eye

  • COX-1 — constitutive; maintains normal corneal epithelium, aqueous production
  • COX-2 — inducible; upregulated in surgical trauma, uveitis, corneal injury
  • Most topical NSAIDs are non-selective (inhibit both). Non-selective inhibition of COX-1 may impair corneal healing — a key safety concern

📋 Key Clinical Uses of Topical NSAIDs

  • Prevention of intra-operative miosis during cataract surgery (prostaglandins cause pupil constriction)
  • Post-cataract surgery inflammation — pain, photophobia, pseudophakic CME prophylaxis
  • Allergic conjunctivitis — ketorolac reduces itch by blocking prostaglandin-mediated itch receptors
  • Refractive surgery (PRK/LASIK) — analgesia without steroid side effects
  • Cystoid macular oedema (CME) — prophylaxis and treatment; often combined with steroids
  • Episcleritis — mild cases respond well to topical NSAIDs alone
  • Seasonal allergic conjunctivitis — ketorolac approved for ocular itch relief
  • Diabetic macular oedema — adjunctive role studied with nepafenac

⚠️ Corneal Toxicity — The Critical Concern

Prolonged or inappropriate NSAID use can cause sterile corneal melt (keratolysis) — a serious, sight-threatening complication. Risk factors include:

  • Pre-existing dry eye disease
  • Corneal epithelial defects
  • Combined use of multiple topical agents
  • Older generation formulations (diclofenac)

🔬 Drug Delivery Innovations

  • Nepafenac (prodrug) — penetrates cornea and is converted to amfenac by intraocular esterases; higher intraocular bioavailability
  • Bromfenac — once-daily dosing due to prolonged tissue binding; contains a bromine atom enhancing potency
  • Flurbiprofen — primarily used intra-operatively to maintain mydriasis during surgery
Mast Cell Stabilizers & Antihistamines

Allergy Mediator
Inhibitors

Allergic eye diseases affect up to 20% of the population. Understanding mast cells, histamine receptors, and dual-action agents is essential for managing these common conditions.

🔬 Mast Cell Biology

Mast cells are concentrated in the conjunctival stroma (~5,000 cells/mm²). They contain granules packed with histamine, tryptase, and other mediators. In allergic disease:

  • Sensitisation: IgE antibodies bind to FcεRI receptors on mast cells upon first antigen exposure
  • Challenge: Re-exposure cross-links IgE → mast cell degranulation
  • Early phase: Histamine, tryptase released within minutes → itch, redness, chemosis
  • Late phase: Eosinophils, neutrophils recruited → chronic inflammation, papillae, giant papillae in VKC

💊 How Mast Cell Stabilizers Work

Sodium cromoglicate and lodoxamide block calcium channels on the mast cell membrane. This prevents the rise in intracellular Ca²⁺ that triggers degranulation. Key points:

  • They do NOT reverse established symptoms — purely prophylactic
  • Must be started 2–4 weeks before allergen season for optimal effect
  • Require frequent dosing (4× daily) — patient compliance is a challenge
  • No risk of IOP elevation or cataract — excellent long-term safety

🧬 Dual-Action Agents — The Modern Approach

Second-generation drugs combine H₁ receptor antagonism (immediate relief) with mast cell stabilisation (long-term prevention). This dual mechanism means they can be started any time, even after symptoms begin.

H₁ Antagonism Component

Blocks histamine from binding H₁ receptors on conjunctival blood vessels and sensory nerve endings → rapid relief of itch and redness within 3–15 minutes

Mast Cell Stabilisation Component

Prevents further mediator release, reducing the late-phase response and recurrence. Also inhibits eosinophil infiltration in chronic disease

Additional Actions of Olopatadine

Inhibits release of prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and tryptase. Olopatadine 0.2% (once-daily) achieves 24-hour itch relief — highest evidence base among all topical allergy agents

Ketotifen — OTC Access

Available over-the-counter in many countries; BID dosing; also inhibits eosinophil activation, making it useful in vernal keratoconjunctivitis (VKC) as adjunctive therapy

🗂️ Grading Allergic Conjunctivitis Severity

  • Mild SAC/PAC — seasonal/perennial; mast cell stabilizer or dual-action agent alone
  • Moderate SAC/PAC — add short course topical steroid (FML or loteprednol); cold compresses, avoid triggers
  • Vernal Keratoconjunctivitis (VKC) — intensive therapy: mast cell stabilizer + topical steroid + lodoxamide; cyclosporin 0.05% for steroid-sparing
  • Atopic Keratoconjunctivitis (AKC) — systemic antihistamines + topical therapy; risk of corneal shield ulcer; tacrolimus

🔄 Steroid-Sparing Strategies

Long-term steroids cause significant side effects. OA students must know these alternatives:

  • Topical ciclosporin 0.05% (Restasis) — calcineurin inhibitor; inhibits T-cell activation; used in dry eye-related inflammation and VKC
  • Topical tacrolimus — more potent calcineurin inhibitor; used off-label in AKC, VKC, and corneal graft rejection prophylaxis
  • Topical NSAIDs — adjunct to reduce steroid dose post-surgically
  • Systemic immunosuppressants — methotrexate, mycophenolate, azathioprine for chronic uveitis

💡 Intravitreal Steroid Implants

  • Ozurdex (dexamethasone 0.7 mg biodegradable) — approved for BRVO, CRVO, non-infectious uveitis, DME; duration ~3–6 months
  • Iluvien (fluocinolone acetonide 0.19 mg) — non-biodegradable; 36 months release; approved for chronic DME
  • Yutiq (fluocinolone acetonide 0.18 mg) — for non-infectious posterior uveitis; 36 months
  • IOP monitoring mandatory with all implants; ~35% require IOP-lowering treatment
Safety Profile

Side Effects & Adverse Effects

Knowing adverse effects is as important as knowing indications. These are high-yield exam and clinical topics.

⭐ Clinical Pearl — Steroid Response

IOP rise (steroid response) occurs in ~5–6% of the general population but in up to 90% of primary open-angle glaucoma patients. High-risk groups: POAG patients, first-degree relatives of glaucoma patients, high myopes, young children, and patients with connective tissue disorders. Always check IOP at 2 and 6 weeks after starting topical steroids.

⭐ Clinical Pearl — Fungal Keratitis

Steroids suppress local immunity. Topical steroids are absolutely contraindicated in active fungal keratitis, active bacterial keratitis, and primary herpetic epithelial keratitis. In interstitial or disciform herpetic keratitis (stromal, non-epithelial), steroids are used WITH concurrent antiviral cover (topical acyclovir or oral valaciclovir).
